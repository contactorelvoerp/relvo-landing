## 1. Value Equation

```
          Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement
Value = ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
                Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice
```

Value grows when you increase the numerator and decrease the denominator. The strongest offers usually win on the denominator — customers pay the most for speed and convenience.

| Lever | Question | How it shows in the copy |
|---|---|---|
| Dream outcome ↑ | What does the customer really want (status, money, peace of mind)? | Headline with a concrete outcome |
| Likelihood ↑ | Why will they believe it works for them? | Testimonials, case studies, method, guarantee |
| Time delay ↓ | How fast is the first result? | "First results in 7 days" |
| Effort ↓ | What do we take off the customer's plate? | "Without …", "we do it for you", templates |

## 2. From problems to solutions

1. Map the entire customer journey: what happens before purchase, at the start, during, and after reaching the outcome.
2. At each stage list the obstacles through the lens of the 4 levers:
   - "This isn't for me / won't work for me" (likelihood)
   - "This will take too long" (time)
   - "This is too hard / I don't have time" (effort)
   - "I'm not sure I care / what the result is" (outcome)
3. Reframe each obstacle as a solution: "How to [achieve X] without [pain Y], even if [constraint Z]".

Target: at least 8 obstacles → 8 solutions. Components and bonuses come from this list.

## 3. Delivery vehicles and Trim & Stack

For each solution, list possible delivery formats:
- Level of involvement: do it yourself (DIY) / done with you (DWY) / done for you (DFY)
- Format: 1:1, group, cohort, community, template, checklist, tool/software, recording, live
- Channel and response time: email, chat, phone; business hours vs. 24/7

**Trim & Stack**: rate each vehicle on two dimensions — delivery cost to the business and value perceived by the customer.
- High cost / low value → cut
- Low cost / high value → keep (core of the stack and the best bonuses)
- High cost / high value → keep in a premium tier or in limited quantity
- Low cost / low value → cut, unless it removes a specific objection

## 4. Value Stack and component valuation

Each component:
- **Name** — attractive and descriptive (not "Module 3" but "The First 10 Clients Map")
- **Outcome** — what the customer gains from it
- **Valuation** — justified: what the market alternative would cost, time saved × value of an hour, money it helps earn/save

Rules:
- Valuations must be credible and defensible. Don't inflate absurdly — losing credibility lowers likelihood (lever 2).
- Total stack value = at least 5–10× the price.
- Present the stack in this order: core → supporting components → bonuses → guarantee → total value summary → price.

## 5. Bonuses

- Each bonus solves a specific obstacle or objection — ideally one that appears right before or right after purchase.
- A bonus helps reach the goal faster or more easily (implementation, templates, kickoff consultation, ready-made scripts, checklists, tool access).
- Each bonus: name, outcome, valuation. Total bonus value ≥ core offer value.
- Bonuses can be limited in time or quantity — a natural, honest source of urgency ("bonus for the first 20 people").
- Good bonuses are often things the business already has or can deliver cheaply and repeatedly.

## 6. Offer naming — MAGIC

- **M**agnetic reason why — why the offer exists now (e.g. "Spring enrollment", "New studio opening")
- **A**vatar — for whom ("for beauty salon owners")
- **G**oal — the outcome ("20 new clients")
- **I**nterval — timeframe ("in 30 days")
- **C**ontainer word — the wrapper ("Program", "Bootcamp", "System", "Challenge", "Accelerator")

Use at least 3 of 5. Write the name in the copy language so it sounds native, not like a literal translation from English. Example (EN): "The 30-Day 20-Client Accelerator for Beauty Salons". Example (PL): "Akcelerator 20 Klientek w 30 Dni — dla salonów beauty".
