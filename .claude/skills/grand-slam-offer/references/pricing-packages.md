# Price, Packages and Pricing Psychology

## Overriding rule: value, not price
- Don't compete on price. You win by making the offer incomparable to anything else on the market (a "category of one"), so the customer has nothing to compare the price against.
- Set the price from the value of the outcome to the customer. Production cost only defines the floor for profitability.
- When tempted to discount — add value instead: a bonus, more units, faster delivery, a stronger guarantee.

## Quantity package eligibility

Packages like "buy 4, get 6" / "buy 2, get 1 free" make sense when at least one condition holds:
- The product is consumable or bought repeatedly (cosmetics, supplements, coffee, pet food)
- The service consists of repeatable units (sessions, workouts, treatments, hours, licenses, seats)
- The outcome requires a series (e.g. 6 treatments produce the result, 1 doesn't)
- The customer buys for multiple people/locations (team, gifts, branches)
- The marginal cost of an additional unit is low (digital, licenses)

Usually not eligible: one-off custom services (website, audit, project), durable goods bought once. Use tiers, installments, and price presentation instead.

Record the decision and rationale in the brief.

## Package mechanisms

| Mechanism | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Buy X, get Y | "Buy 4 sessions, get 6" | Stronger than a percentage discount — communicates a gain, not a price cut |
| Buy X, get 1 free | "Buy 2, get 1 free" | Simple, understood in a second |
| Outcome package | "Full series of 6 treatments = guaranteed result" | Ties the package to an outcome guarantee |
| Package with bonus | "With the 6-pack — free kickoff consultation" | Adds value instead of cutting price |
| Subscription with premium | "Annual plan = 2 months free" | Rewards commitment |

## Tiers (good / better / best)
- 3 options; the middle or top one marked as recommended ("Most popular").
- The most expensive option as an **anchor** — makes the recommended one feel reasonable. It must be real and sellable, not a dummy.
- **Decoy**: an option only slightly cheaper than the recommended one but clearly weaker — makes choosing the recommended one easy.
- Describe differences between tiers by outcome and level of support (DIY → DWY → DFY), not by lists of technical features.
- Quantity packages can be combined with tiers (e.g. 1 / 4+2 / 8+4).

## Price presentation
- **Value anchor**: "Total value: $12,400. Your investment: $1,990."
- **Cost of the problem**: "Every month without a system costs you roughly $8,000 in lost deals."
- **Breakdown**: per day, per session, per client, per result ("less than the value of a single client").
- **Installments vs. one-time**: one-time payment with a premium (bonus or lower total), installments for accessibility.
- "Investment" instead of "cost" for B2B and education offers.
- Match price endings to positioning: premium → round numbers (2,000), mass market → charm endings (1,990).
- Use the currency, number format, and price conventions of the copy language/market (e.g. "1 990 zł", "1.990 €", "$1,990").

## Price reductions in the EU
If you still communicate a price reduction, the Omnibus Directive requires showing the lowest price from the 30 days before the reduction. Tell the user. Adding value (bonuses, extra units) is not a price reduction and is usually more effective.

## Forbidden
- "Cheapest", "lowest price", "cheaper than …", price comparisons with competitors as the main argument (and their equivalents in any language)
- Price in the hero as a hook
- Fictitious "original prices" and struck-through amounts without backing
